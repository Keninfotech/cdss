# Clinical Knowledge Base (RAG corpus)

Each section below is one retrievable chunk. The id in square brackets is cited by the
safety rules and by the recommendation engine. Content is a condensed educational summary
of widely cited guidance; always confirm against the current full guideline before use.

## [G-FQ] Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) - safety
Source: US FDA Drug Safety Communications (2016, 2018); EMA referral (2018).
Fluoroquinolones carry warnings for disabling and potentially permanent adverse effects of
tendons, muscles, joints, nerves and the CNS. Tendon rupture risk is higher in people over
60, those on corticosteroids, and transplant recipients. They can cause serious
dysglycaemia (hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia), especially in older diabetic patients
taking oral hypoglycaemics (sulfonylureas) or insulin; hypoglycaemic coma has been
reported. They may prolong the QT interval and lower the seizure threshold. Divalent and
trivalent cations (calcium, magnesium, iron, antacids) reduce absorption. Reserve for
infections where alternatives are unsuitable.

## [G-HYPO] Hypoglycaemia risk with antidiabetic combinations
Source: ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes (2025), Section 6 and 13.
Insulin and insulin secretagogues (sulfonylureas such as glimepiride and gliclazide) are
the main causes of drug-induced hypoglycaemia. Risk rises with age, renal or hepatic
impairment, irregular meals and interacting drugs. In older adults, glycaemic targets
should be individualised and agents with low hypoglycaemia risk (metformin, DPP-4
inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 RAs) preferred. Beta-blockers may blunt adrenergic
warning symptoms such as tremor and palpitations; sweating is usually preserved. Patients
on insulin or sulfonylureas should be asked about hypoglycaemia at every visit.

## [G-NSAID] NSAIDs - bleeding and cardiovascular-renal risk
Source: NICE CKS NSAIDs prescribing issues; AGS Beers Criteria (2023).
NSAIDs (diclofenac, ibuprofen, naproxen, aceclofenac) increase the risk of GI ulceration
and bleeding; the risk is multiplied by concurrent anticoagulants, antiplatelets (aspirin,
clopidogrel), corticosteroids and SSRIs. NSAIDs raise blood pressure, cause sodium and
fluid retention and can worsen heart failure. Use the lowest effective dose for the
shortest time, add a PPI for patients at GI risk, and prefer paracetamol or topical NSAIDs
for musculoskeletal pain in older adults.

## [G-AKI] Triple whammy and drug-induced acute kidney injury
Source: Think Kidneys / NHS England AKI programme; Lapi et al., BMJ 2013.
Concurrent use of a diuretic, an ACE inhibitor or ARB, and an NSAID ("triple whammy")
increases the risk of acute kidney injury, with the highest risk in the first 30 days of
the combination. Spironolactone with an ACE inhibitor or ARB increases the risk of
hyperkalaemia. In dehydration or acute illness (vomiting, diarrhoea, fever) consider
temporarily withholding these drugs ("sick day rules") and check creatinine and potassium.

## [G-STEROID] Corticosteroid-induced hyperglycaemia
Source: Joint British Diabetes Societies (JBDS) guidance on steroid use in diabetes.
Systemic corticosteroids, including single depot intramuscular or intra-articular
injections such as methylprednisolone acetate 80 mg, can raise blood glucose for several
days to a few weeks, mainly in the afternoon and evening. People with diabetes should
increase capillary glucose monitoring after steroid exposure and may need temporary
treatment escalation. Steroids also impair wound healing and may mask infection.

## [G-CNS] Opioids with gabapentinoids or benzodiazepines
Source: US FDA Drug Safety Communications (2016 boxed warning for opioid + benzodiazepine;
2019 warning for gabapentinoids with CNS depressants).
Combining opioids (including tramadol) with benzodiazepines or with gabapentinoids
(pregabalin, gabapentin) increases the risk of profound sedation, respiratory depression,
coma and death. Elderly patients and those with respiratory disease are at highest risk.
Use the lowest effective doses for the shortest duration, avoid alcohol and counsel the
patient and carers about drowsiness and breathing problems.

## [G-SERO] Serotonergic combinations and tramadol
Source: MHRA / FDA labelling for tramadol; Boyer & Shannon, NEJM 2005.
Tramadol inhibits serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake. With SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics,
ondansetron or other serotonergic drugs it may cause serotonin syndrome (agitation,
tremor, clonus, hyperthermia, diarrhoea). Tramadol lowers the seizure threshold; the risk
increases with other seizure-threshold-lowering drugs such as fluoroquinolones,
tricyclics and SSRIs. Tramadol-paracetamol combinations (e.g. 37.5/325 mg) add to total
daily paracetamol intake.

## [G-QT] QT prolongation and domperidone
Source: EMA PRAC review of domperidone (2014); CredibleMeds QT drug lists.
Domperidone is associated with a small increased risk of serious ventricular arrhythmia
and sudden cardiac death. It is contraindicated with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as
clarithromycin, ketoconazole) and with other QT-prolonging drugs, and should be used at
the lowest dose (max 10 mg up to three times daily) for no longer than one week.
Clarithromycin, azithromycin, fluoroquinolones, ondansetron, escitalopram and tricyclic
antidepressants can all prolong QT; combinations warrant ECG and electrolyte checks in
high-risk patients.

## [G-LIVER] Prescribing in chronic liver disease
Source: EASL Clinical Practice Guidelines on decompensated cirrhosis (2018); BNF hepatic
impairment guidance.
Paracetamol is the analgesic of choice in chronic liver disease at a reduced maximum of
about 2-3 g/day (2 g/day if malnourished, alcohol use or cirrhosis). NSAIDs should be
avoided because of GI bleeding, renal impairment and diuretic resistance. Benzodiazepines
and opioids can precipitate hepatic encephalopathy. Sulfonylureas carry a higher risk of
prolonged hypoglycaemia; metformin should be avoided in decompensated disease. Insulin
requirements may change as liver function changes.

## [G-BEERS] AGS Beers Criteria (2023) - selected items
Source: American Geriatrics Society 2023 Updated Beers Criteria.
For adults aged 65 and over, avoid where possible: chronic non-COX-selective NSAIDs
(unless alternatives fail, with gastroprotection); benzodiazepines (falls, fractures,
cognitive impairment, delirium); strongly anticholinergic drugs including tricyclic
antidepressants such as amitriptyline; sulfonylureas as first- or second-line therapy
(hypoglycaemia - if used, choose short-acting agents); aspirin for primary prevention of
cardiovascular disease. Use with caution: opioids with gabapentinoids, and drugs that
cause SIADH or hyponatraemia (SSRIs, diuretics) - monitor sodium.

## [G-THYROID] Levothyroxine administration
Source: Levothyroxine product information; American Thyroid Association guidance.
Levothyroxine should be taken on an empty stomach, 30-60 minutes before breakfast.
Calcium, iron, magnesium, aluminium antacids and bile-acid binders reduce absorption and
should be separated by at least 4 hours. PPIs may reduce absorption. Alternating doses
(e.g. 150 mcg on weekdays and 125 mcg at weekends) are an accepted way of fine-tuning the
weekly dose. TSH should be rechecked 6-8 weeks after any dose change.

## [G-DUP] Therapeutic duplication
Source: ISMP / WHO Medication Without Harm (2017).
Therapeutic duplication - two drugs of the same class, or the same ingredient in two
products (for example metformin in two combination tablets, two calcium-channel
blockers, or paracetamol in both a plain tablet and a tramadol combination) - is a common
preventable error when patients see several prescribers. Medication reconciliation at
every transition of care should identify the intended regimen and stop duplicates.

## [G-CELL] Cellulitis management
Source: NICE NG141 Cellulitis and erysipelas: antimicrobial prescribing (2019); IDSA SSTI
guideline (2014).
Mark the extent of erythema, consider underlying conditions (diabetes, venous
insufficiency, lymphoedema, oedema, tinea pedis) and elevate the limb. First-line oral
antibiotics target streptococci and staphylococci (e.g. flucloxacillin; clarithromycin,
doxycycline or clindamycin in penicillin allergy) for 5-7 days, extended if not improving.
Reassess if symptoms worsen or do not improve within 2-3 days. Refer urgently or admit
for: systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, confusion), rapidly spreading
infection, suspected necrotising fasciitis, severe pain out of proportion, or orbital
involvement. Recurrent lower-limb cellulitis is associated with venous disease;
compression therapy after resolution and skin care reduce recurrence.

## [G-DM-FOOT] Diabetes, infection and the lower limb
Source: IWGDF/IDSA guidelines on diabetic foot infection (2023).
In people with diabetes, lower-limb soft-tissue infection may progress quickly and
signs can be blunted. Check glycaemic control (capillary glucose, HbA1c), peripheral
pulses and sensation. Hyperglycaemia impairs neutrophil function and healing; acute
infection itself raises glucose. Urgent referral if there is systemic infection,
ischaemia, deep abscess or gangrene.

## [G-VEIN] Varicose veins and compression
Source: NICE CG168 Varicose veins: diagnosis and management.
Refer people with symptomatic varicose veins, skin changes or venous leg ulcers to a
vascular service; duplex ultrasound confirms reflux. Compression hosiery is used for
symptom relief and after an episode of venous-related skin complication, once infection
has resolved and arterial supply is confirmed adequate.

## [G-OA] Knee osteoarthritis - pain management
Source: NICE NG226 Osteoarthritis in over 16s (2022).
Offer exercise and weight management as core treatment. Topical NSAIDs are first-line
drug treatment for knee osteoarthritis. Consider oral NSAIDs at the lowest dose for the
shortest time with a PPI, taking GI, renal and cardiovascular risk into account. Do not
routinely offer paracetamol or weak opioids alone unless used infrequently for short-term
pain relief. Intra-articular corticosteroid injection may give short-term relief.
Glucosamine and chondroitin are not recommended by NICE.

## [G-POSTOP-SPINE] After lumbar spine surgery
Source: Standard neurosurgical discharge advice.
Expected: incisional pain improving day by day. Seek review for fever, wound redness,
swelling or discharge, new or worsening leg weakness or numbness, bladder or bowel
dysfunction (possible cauda equina), or severe uncontrolled pain. Short courses of
analgesics (paracetamol, tramadol) and neuropathic agents (pregabalin) are common;
watch for additive sedation.

## [G-HPYLORI] H. pylori eradication and PPIs
Source: Maastricht VI / Florence Consensus (2022); ACG guideline (2024).
Clarithromycin-based triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin) for 14 days is
appropriate only where clarithromycin resistance is low; bismuth quadruple therapy is
preferred elsewhere. Avoid co-prescribing domperidone with clarithromycin (QT risk).
Long-term PPI use should be reviewed periodically.

## [G-ANX] Generalised anxiety disorder - pharmacological treatment
Source: NICE CG113 Generalised anxiety disorder and panic disorder.
An SSRI (e.g. sertraline, escitalopram) is first-line drug therapy. Benzodiazepines should
not be used beyond short-term crisis management (2-4 weeks) because of dependence.
Review early for increased agitation and suicidal thoughts, especially at the start of
treatment. Low-dose tricyclics (e.g. amitriptyline 10 mg for IBS) added later can
interact with SSRIs.

## [G-IBS] Irritable bowel syndrome
Source: NICE CG61 Irritable bowel syndrome in adults.
Antispasmodics (e.g. mebeverine) are first-line for pain/bloating. Low-dose tricyclic
antidepressants (amitriptyline 5-10 mg at night, titrated) are second-line for pain; SSRIs
only if TCAs are ineffective. Review red flags: weight loss, rectal bleeding, anaemia,
age over 60 with change in bowel habit.

## [G-DERM] Topical acne therapy
Source: AAD Guidelines of care for acne vulgaris (2024).
Benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene) and topical antibiotics are
core treatments. Topical antibiotics (clindamycin) should not be used as monotherapy -
combine with benzoyl peroxide to limit resistance. Benzoyl peroxide can oxidise tretinoin
when applied together; apply at separate times. Retinoids, glycolic acid and benzoyl
peroxide are irritant and increase photosensitivity; use moisturiser and daily sunscreen.

## [G-VITD] Vitamin D replacement
Source: Endocrine Society guideline (2024); NICE CKS vitamin D deficiency.
Oral loading regimens (e.g. 60,000 IU weekly for 8 weeks) are commonly used in India.
Very high single intramuscular doses (e.g. 600,000 IU) combined with ongoing oral loading
can lead to hypervitaminosis D and hypercalcaemia; check serum calcium if symptoms
(nausea, thirst, polyuria, confusion) occur and avoid duplicate loading.

## [G-GOUT] Urate-lowering therapy
Source: ACR Guideline for the Management of Gout (2020).
Febuxostat is an alternative to allopurinol; start low and titrate to a serum urate
target below 6 mg/dL, with anti-inflammatory prophylaxis for the first 3-6 months. In
patients with established cardiovascular disease, allopurinol is preferred because of a
cardiovascular-death signal with febuxostat (CARES trial).

## [G-ASPIRIN] Low-dose aspirin in older adults
Source: USPSTF (2022); ASPREE trial (NEJM 2018).
Low-dose aspirin is recommended for secondary prevention after MI, stroke or established
atherosclerotic disease. For primary prevention in adults 60 years or older, initiation is
not recommended because bleeding risk outweighs benefit. Consider gastroprotection in
older adults on long-term aspirin.

## [G-TRIAGE] Triage and early warning scores
Source: Royal College of Physicians NEWS2 (2017); WHO Emergency Triage Assessment and
Treatment (ETAT).
NEWS2 scores respiratory rate, SpO2, systolic BP, pulse, consciousness and temperature.
Aggregate score 0-4 = low risk (routine), a score of 3 in any single parameter or 5-6 =
urgent clinical review, 7 or more = emergency response / transfer. Emergency signs include
obstructed breathing, severe respiratory distress, shock, convulsions, unconsciousness and
severe dehydration. Blood glucose below 54 mg/dL or with confusion is an emergency;
above 300 mg/dL with symptoms requires same-day review.

## [G-DISCHARGE-REDFLAGS] Discharge red-flag symptoms (St. John's discharge template)
Source: St. John's Medical College Hospital discharge summary (patient P001).
Report to the emergency department 24/7 for: severe unbearable pain anywhere, bleeding
anywhere, convulsions, unresponsiveness, loss of vision, inability to drink liquids,
vomiting, diarrhoea, breathlessness, palpitations, jaundice, absence of urine, fever,
rash, joint swelling, behavioural change including violence, self-harm or agitation, and
weakness or fatigue.

## [G-PAIN-OPTIONS] Choosing an analgesic - patient factors
Source: WHO analgesic ladder (adapted); NICE CKS analgesia.
Paracetamol is first-line for mild-moderate pain in most patients (reduce ceiling in liver
disease and low body weight). Topical NSAIDs are useful for localised musculoskeletal
pain with minimal systemic exposure. Oral NSAIDs should be avoided with anticoagulants,
CKD, heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, ARB/ACE + diuretic therapy and in the
elderly where possible. Weak opioids (tramadol) carry risks of sedation, serotonin
syndrome, seizures and falls.

## [G-FEVER-OPTIONS] Fever - first steps in primary care
Source: NICE NG143 / NG51 (sepsis) - adapted.
Look for a source and screen for sepsis (temperature, heart rate, BP, SpO2, mental
state). In a patient with a recent wound, cellulitis or surgery, fever may indicate
spreading infection and needs same-day review. Paracetamol is the usual antipyretic.
Patients on immunosuppressants or with diabetes may present atypically.

## [G-GI-OPTIONS] Nausea, vomiting and dyspepsia
Source: NICE CKS dyspepsia and nausea/vomiting summaries.
Persistent vomiting with inability to keep fluids down needs urgent assessment and
temporary review of metformin, diuretics, ARB/ACE inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors (sick
day rules). Ondansetron prolongs QT. Domperidone should be short-term and avoided with
QT-prolonging drugs or CYP3A4 inhibitors. A PPI is first-line for dyspepsia; review need
for long-term PPI.
